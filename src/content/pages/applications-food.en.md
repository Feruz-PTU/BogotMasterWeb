Licorice extract is available too, made by our extraction partner. This page sets out the forms, the EU and US rules food buyers work under, and the documents we can give you.

## Where licorice goes in food {#uses}

The regulations themselves show where licorice is used. The EU's contaminant rules name dried liquorice root "including as an ingredient in herbal infusions", and liquorice extract "for use in food in particular beverages and confectionary". The US rule sets limits for licorice in baked goods, alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks, chewing gum, hard and soft candy, herbs and seasonings, and plant protein products.

The root goes into teas and infusions, and into dishes cooked with it. The extract goes into sweets and drinks.

## EU rules {#eu}

### "Contains liquorice" on the label {#eu-labelling}

Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information requires a statement on confectionery and beverages that contain glycyrrhizinic acid or its ammonium salt, added as such or through the liquorice plant *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (Annex III, point 3):

| Food | Glycyrrhizinic acid, from | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| Confectionery or beverages | 100 mg/kg or 10 mg/l | "contains liquorice", immediately after the list of ingredients, unless "liquorice" is already in that list or in the name of the food |
| Confectionery | 4 g/kg | "contains liquorice – people suffering from hypertension should avoid excessive consumption" |
| Beverages | 50 mg/l, or 300 mg/l for beverages with more than 1.2 % alcohol by volume | The same statement |

The levels apply to the product as offered ready for consumption, or as reconstituted according to the manufacturer's instructions. Without a list of ingredients, the statement goes with the name of the food.

### Flavouring substances {#eu-flavourings}

Glycyrrhizic acid (FL No 16.012) and ammoniated glycyrrhizic acid (FL No 16.060) are on the Union list of flavouring substances, Annex I to Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008, each with maximum levels by food category.

### Ochratoxin A {#eu-ochratoxin}

Ochratoxin A is a toxin made by some moulds. Regulation (EU) 2023/915 sets these maximum levels for liquorice (Annex I, entry 1.2.19):

| Product | Maximum level of ochratoxin A |
|---|---|
| Liquorice root (dried), including as an ingredient in herbal infusions | 20 µg/kg |
| Liquorice extract for use in food, in particular beverages and confectionery | 80 µg/kg |
| Liquorice confectionery containing at least 97 % liquorice extract on a dry basis | 50 µg/kg |
| Other liquorice confectionery | 10 µg/kg |

The extract limit applies to the pure, undiluted extract; the regulation notes that 1 kg of extract is obtained from 3 to 4 kg of liquorice root.

### Pesticide residues {#eu-pesticides}

Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 sets the maximum residue levels of pesticides in food; see our [import rules chapter](/buyers-guide/import-and-hs-codes#eu).

## US rules {#us}

Under 21 CFR 184.1408, licorice, licorice extract, ammoniated glycyrrhizin and monoammonium glycyrrhizinate are affirmed as generally recognised as safe (GRAS), within these limits on the glycyrrhizin content of the food as served:

| Food category | Maximum glycyrrhizin, % of the food as served |
|---|---|
| Baked foods | 0.05 |
| Alcoholic beverages | 0.1 |
| Non-alcoholic beverages | 0.15 |
| Chewing gum | 1.1 |
| Hard candy | 16.0 |
| Herbs and seasonings | 0.15 |
| Plant protein products | 0.15 |
| Soft candy | 3.1 |
| Vitamin or mineral dietary supplements | 0.5 |
| All other foods, except sugar substitutes | 0.1 |

The same section does not permit these ingredients as a non-nutritive sweetener in sugar substitutes. It also sets ash, acid-insoluble ash, heavy metal and arsenic limits for licorice root; they are compared with the pharmacopoeias on our [pharmaceutical page](/applications/pharmaceutical#pharmacopoeias).

## Why the EU labelling rule exists {#why}

The statements were introduced by Commission Directive 2004/77/EC and carried into Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. The Directive cites the opinion of the EU's Scientific Committee on Food of 4 April 2003, which set an upper limit of 100 mg a day for regular intake of glycyrrhizinic acid. Its stated aim is to give consumers "clear information on the presence of glycyrrhizinic acid or its ammonium salt in confectionery and beverages".
